CCNA Learning Path

The structured study, practical lab work and technical resources I am using to build networking knowledge and prepare for the Cisco CCNA.

My approach to learning networking combines structured courses with practical configuration, troubleshooting and documentation. The aim is not simply to recognise CCNA concepts, but to understand how they affect the behaviour of an actual network.

Current study plan

I am continuing to build my networking knowledge through a combination of structured CCNA study, browser-based and simulated lab environments, practice exams, vendor documentation and independent troubleshooting.

  • PingMyNetwork — interactive CCNA study, practical Cisco CLI labs, troubleshooting exercises, practice exams and independent Sandbox work
  • Jeremy’s IT Lab — structured CCNA teaching, Packet Tracer exercises and detailed technical explanations
  • NetworkChuck Academy — completed the Summer of CCNA programme, which provided an important foundation for the practical work that followed
  • Cisco official documentation — configuration references, protocol behaviour and platform-specific information
  • Hands-on lab practice — Cisco Packet Tracer, PingMyNetwork labs and Sandbox environments, Cisco IOS CLI and increasingly larger troubleshooting scenarios
  • Independent documentation — recording configuration, verification, failures, troubleshooting and lessons learned throughout this portfolio

Study approach

I have found that networking concepts become much easier to retain when theory is followed immediately by practical use.

  1. Learn the concept — work through a structured lesson or technical explanation.
  2. Summarise the important points — reduce the subject to the behaviour, terminology and commands that matter.
  3. Build it — configure the technology in a practical lab rather than relying entirely on theoretical understanding.
  4. Verify it — use show commands, connectivity tests and protocol state to prove that the configuration behaves as expected.
  5. Break and troubleshoot it — investigate mistakes, deliberate faults and unexpected behaviour rather than immediately rebuilding the configuration.
  6. Document the result — record the configuration, evidence, problems encountered and lessons learned.
  7. Revisit the subject — use repetition, practice questions and mixed troubleshooting exercises to strengthen recall.
THE OBJECTIVE

Develop strong networking fundamentals aligned with the CCNA while building the practical troubleshooting habits needed to understand why a network works, or why it does not.

I completed the Summer of CCNA programme through NetworkChuck Academy. It provided a broad introduction to the technologies covered by the certification and established an important foundation for the practical work documented throughout this portfolio.

Teaching from Chuck Keith, Jeremy Cioara and Zach Hill helped connect networking theory with practical examples and gave me a structured starting point from which to continue developing independently.

PingMyNetwork

PingMyNetwork has become an important part of my continuing CCNA study, particularly because it combines short technical explanations with immediate practical use of the Cisco CLI.

The gamified structure, practical labs and practice exams have been useful for identifying areas where I recognise a subject but cannot yet recall or apply it confidently without assistance.

OSPF is a good example. It was a subject I initially understood more comfortably in theory than in practice. Repeated configuration, verification and troubleshooting exercises have helped turn that into a much more practical understanding.

More recently, I have also been using the PingMyNetwork Sandbox to create my own network scenarios rather than only completing existing exercises.

PingMyNetwork Labs and Sandbox

PingMyNetwork provides another practical environment for working directly with Cisco-style CLI configuration and troubleshooting.

The structured labs are useful for repeated practice, while the Sandbox allows me to move beyond predefined tasks and construct my own topologies and fault scenarios.

This became particularly useful while creating Lab 074 — The Branch Office OSPF Blackout .

For that exercise I first created and verified a working three-router OSPF network, then deliberately introduced an area mismatch, an incorrectly passive router-to-router interface and a missing network advertisement.

Designing the faults required thinking about what the learner should observe, which commands would expose each problem and how the symptoms should change as each stage was corrected.

That made creating the lab a useful learning exercise in its own right.

From solving labs to designing them

A useful change in the way I am studying has been moving beyond only completing exercises written by somebody else.

Designing my own troubleshooting scenario required a different way of thinking about the network.

Rather than simply asking:

WHAT IS BROKEN?

I had to decide what should remain operational, what should fail, which symptoms each fault should produce and what evidence should allow another learner to identify the cause.

In the OSPF Sandbox lab, the intended troubleshooting progression was:

  1. Verify that the physical interfaces remain operational.
  2. Discover that the expected OSPF neighbours have not formed.
  3. Correct an OSPF area mismatch.
  4. Identify an incorrectly passive WAN interface.
  5. Restore both OSPF adjacencies.
  6. Recognise that FULL neighbour relationships do not automatically mean every required route is being advertised.
  7. Restore the missing Branch LAN advertisement.
  8. Verify bidirectional end-to-end connectivity.

Creating that sequence helped reinforce the difference between basic IP connectivity, OSPF neighbour formation, route advertisement and final end-to-end reachability.

Why document the learning process?

The portfolio is deliberately more than a collection of successful configurations.

Recording mistakes, verification commands and troubleshooting steps makes it possible to look back at how my understanding has changed. It also forces me to explain what a configuration is doing rather than treating a successful ping as sufficient evidence that I understand it.

That distinction has become increasingly important as the labs have moved from isolated commands into networks where switching, routing, services and security controls interact.

Keeping raw CLI evidence alongside the main lab write-ups also provides a record of the actual device state rather than relying only on a description of what should have happened.

THE LONG-TERM GOAL

Build enough technical understanding that configuration commands are the result of understanding the network rather than a set of steps remembered from a lesson.

CCNA certification is an important milestone, but the larger objective is to develop practical networking knowledge and troubleshooting habits that can continue to be built upon afterwards.
